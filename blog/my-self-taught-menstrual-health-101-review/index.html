My self-taught Menstrual Health 101 review

November 24, 2026 · Menstrual health

I'm not new to menstrual health as a patient. I have a long history of symptoms being dismissed, a diagnosis that came years late, and major surgery to "fix" my period.

But I never really learned about it. The information wasn't there when I scrolled my social media. The school, doctor, or others never told me this was something I should learn. Honestly, I thought it was just me, that I had done something wrong to make myself suffer.

In recent years, talking with more women and seeing them go through their own menstrual health issues and struggles, I started to realize it was never just me, and the problem never happened only to me. Along the way, as I educated myself about menstrual health, three things I watched, listened to and read were super helpful and informative. I want to share them here so we know how serious these issues are and that we're not alone. Maybe they'll help you understand your own body, start a conversation with your daughter or mom, or push back the next time someone says it's normal.

1. Watch: End of the Cycle

Earlier this month I saw End of the Cycle, a documentary about endometriosis, at a screening at University of California, San Francisco. It follows 6 women with very different bodies, careers and ages, going through endometriosis in completely different ways.

Marilyn Monroe.

This is the story that shocked me most. Not just because it was the first time I learned she had endometriosis, but also because of how much she went through, ALONE, while still holding a "perfect" figure in front of the public.

She suffered from severe period pain since the age of 16. Her first husband remembered that during her periods she would ask him to stop the car, then lie on the grass by the road, "writhing in pain." Years later (after she passed away), people found 14 boxes of pills in her dressing room, almost all painkillers for menstrual cramps.

As the most famous woman of her era, the press and public were extremely critical of her every move. When her films ran over budget, she was the one questioned. Newspapers ran the headline “Appendectomy To Cost Film Studio,” while few people cared that “MM in Pain; Doctors Await Test Results.” When her movies ran over schedule, even when she collapsed right after finishing Some Like It Hot, she was still called “unprofessional” by the press and actors who worked with her.

Monroe said: "I was sick enough to go to a hospital and I read that I am holding up production."

This makes me think: how do we measure the success and productivity for women professionals at work, considering we have different bodies and function differently?

Brittany Brown.

She won Olympic bronze in the 200m in Paris while in pain, then collapsed on her victory lap and missed every interview of the biggest moment of her career. How many doctors did she see before a diagnosis? "Maybe seven or eight." Over how long? "A decade."

This makes me think: if an Olympic athlete, someone whose body is examined constantly, needs a decade and over seven doctors to get an endometriosis diagnosis, what does it take for a normal woman like you and me?

Linda Griffith.

Linda Griffith is an MIT professor. It's both a little "funny" and very shocking to learn that she fainted from her periods as a girl and her mother gave her gin "to make me stop crying." At Berkeley, she was taking 30 ibuprofen pills a day during her period.

She didn't realize it wasn't normal until one day, while writing her thesis, she couldn't even turn around to answer a ringing phone. A labmate came over and said, "Hey, something must be really wrong with you if you can't answer the phone." Years later, she also survived triple-negative breast cancer, and called it "a walk in the park compared to endometriosis."

She turned her pain into a force to advance menstruation science for everyone: she built an endometriosis research center at MIT, and she is pushing the truth, and a cure, a bit closer every day. I admire her, and I see myself in her. 🩵

Watch it at endocollective.org. It's free, with a suggested $5 donation; profits go to research.

2. Listen: Huberman Lab with Dr. Thaïs Aliabadi

Andrew Huberman and OB-GYN Dr. Thais Aliabadi spend three hours on PMOS (formerly PCOS), endometriosis, fertility, perimenopause and breast cancer: the common symptoms of each, why they so often get missed, and what you can do about them. It's long, but worth it for the education. What I took away:

Painful periods are NOT normal.

Cramps that two Advil fix are fine. But if, because of your period, you miss school or work, stay in bed, cancel plans or end up in urgent care, that isn't normal. Pain that keeps coming back and gets worse over time is probably "endometriosis until proven otherwise."

If men had the same thing, it would have been considered a national emergency.

I love the part where Dr. Aliabadi asked Andrew to imagine men had a condition that caused severe pain during sex, scarred their scrotums, lowered their sperm count, was the top cause of their infertility, kept them in bed two or three days a month, and sent them to the ER a few times a year. "Do you think majority of them would go undiagnosed?" Andrew's answer: "No. The problem would be dealt with very differently." He referred to how fast drugs like Viagra get approved. "It would have been considered a national emergency."

Pay a little more attention to the teens around you.

Signs of PMOS and endometriosis often start in the teens, when they're easy to dismiss as "just puberty." If your daughter, sister or friend is missing school because of her period, has acne that won't clear, or has a hard time with food, pay a little more attention. If in doubt, take them to see a doctor.

Most doctors want to help, so guide them at your next visit.

Often "you only have 10 minutes with your doctor", so go prepared and help them better listen to you. Before you go, write down your symptoms and how they affect your life. In the room, state them plainly and completely. Then ask questions, like "Could this be endometriosis?", and ask for tests: a pelvic ultrasound and an AMH (egg count) test. If one doctor says no, see another one. Keep pushing until you get answers.

I really appreciate Andrew's curiosity, his questions, and his care and support for this topic. It shows what it looks like when a man gets involved. Women's health problems are not only the woman's problem. They affect everyone around her - her partner, her son, her family and her colleagues.

Note: this episode came out in November 2025. Some things have changed since then, like PCOS being renamed PMOS, and research and technology keep advancing. Also, Dr. Aliabadi sells some of the supplements she recommends on the show. If you want to act on any specific detail, do a bit of your own research or check with your doctor.

Listen on Huberman Lab or your own Podcast platform.

3. Read: Period Repair Manual by Lara Briden

An OB-GYN and a close friend both recommended told me, separately, that I had to read this. I'm partway through, and it's my Thanksgiving book. Read it with me. 🤓

What I've enjoyed so far:

Listen to your period.

I love that the book starts by helping you understand your period: what it should be like, what can go wrong, and where hormonal birth control fits in. As English philosopher Francis Bacon said, "knowledge itself is power." With more understanding, there is much less to be feared. And without the fear, we can deal with it proactively and plan our lives better.

I also love that she says your period is trying to tell you something. It's a messenger of your overall health, just like our emotions! So when your period is bad, don't get mad at it. Treat it as your "monthly report card" of how your body has been doing. Even the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) calls the menstrual cycle a "vital sign."

Maintain your period to set a good foundation, then treat specifically.

The second part of the book starts with general maintenance for your period, setting up a good foundation before moving on to targeted treatment for specific problems, like PMOS, PMS and period pain. My favorite tips: rest and joy are the best treatments for stress, so put what you love on your calendar, like a meeting with yourself. And if you have to choose between sleep and exercise, "choose sleep!"

Natural ways to improve symptoms.

Before you reach for birth control, painkillers or other pills, there are a lot of natural ways to improve your symptoms: what you eat, supplements like magnesium (the "miracle mineral for periods"), and other things. But from my own experience, these are definitely not a quick fix. You have to stick with them consistently to see and keep the change.

I'll share a more detailed review once I finish the whole book.

Note: a new edition of this book will come out this December, with new research and clinical insights. If you haven't bought it yet, it may be worth the wait. It would also make a great holiday gift for the girls and women you love. (Not sponsored!😅)

Read it from your local library (the e-book is on Libby) or grab a copy from a bookstore. Find more of Lara Briden's writing on her website.
