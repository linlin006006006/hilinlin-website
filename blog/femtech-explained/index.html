Femtech, explained

September 20, 2026 · Femtech

After Fintech, Edtech, Biotech, Deeptech, and every other “XXtech” — it is time to welcome and embrace the Femtech era.

In short, Femtech is the technology that improves the health and wellness for women, females, and girls.

The audience of Femtech includes:

  • Females: usually refers to biological sex, assigned at birth
  • Women: usually refers to gender identity, which doesn't always match biological sex
  • Girls: young humans who are female

Interestingly, girls are often forgotten in Femtech. Maybe that's because, for a long time, when people pictured "children," they pictured little human who are genderless. But girls have genitals and hormones too. As their bodies grow and mature, they face the same reproductive and hormonal issues women do. I doubt it's news to you that someone missed school in middle or high school because of severe menstrual pain or even dropped out of the school because of pregnancy.

Femtech companies tackle challenges including reproductive, menstrual, sexual, pelvic floor, vaginal, oncological, bone, brain, autoimmune, heart health, etc. It also covers mental health conditions tied to female genitals and hormones, like PMS-related depression. Fertility care and menopause have been the two hottest areas in recent years.

One thing I found fascinating recently: men and women can get different symptoms from the same disease, or from doing the same thing.

We've all seen the television and movie scenes of a person having a heart attack: someone, usually a guy, suddenly clutches his chest, in extreme pain and then fall down on the ground - it makes for good drama with its dramatic forms. And for men, it's often accurate.

But for women, it often isn't.

Women are more likely to have subtler signs when they experience heart attacks: extreme fatigue, shortness of breath, nausea, back or jaw discomfort. That makes a heart attack much easier to miss and harder to diagnose.

That also applies to fitness. When men train and eat with extreme discipline, they build muscle. When women do the same, they can lose their periods.

So let's go back to basics: males and females are different - from cell level.

Did you know that, historically, drug development, disease diagnosis, and medical benchmarks were built mostly on male bodies and male cells?

Did you know that, it wasn't until 1993 that women were allowed to early-phase drug trials?

Did you know that, it wasn't until 2016 that NIH required grant applicants to account for sex as a biological variable?

I've had severe menstrual pain since I was 13. Even after the pain sent me to the hospital, my experience was dismissed, because that's the culture I was in. Later, I lost my period for a year and a half, and my OB-GYN just kept telling me to take birth control. And that wasn't the only time.

At first, I thought it was just my personal experience. It wasn't until I started talking to the women around me that I realized these moments of pain and discomfort were shared, and normalized as "just part of being a woman."

Women and girls are half the world's population, roughly 4.1 billion people. The women's health market is projected to reach about $1.2 trillion by 2027 (FemTech Focus, 2021). Women already drive most household purchasing decisions in health and wellness. The demand was never in question. The supply was.

Let's build more Femtech, seriously.

If you're building in women's health, or you know someone who is, I'd love to connect. Let's explore solutions we can all benefit from.
